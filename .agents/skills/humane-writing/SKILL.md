# Humane Writing

## Why this matters

A person reading a cover letter or a follow-up email isn't grading it against a rubric — they're forming an impression of whether they'd want to work with this person. AI-generated prose has a recognizable rhythm: it's evenly balanced, reaches for the same handful of intensifiers, and structures almost every paragraph the same way. None of that is wrong, exactly, but a reader who's seen a thousand cover letters this year notices the pattern before they notice the content. The goal here isn't to trick anyone — it's to write the way the user would actually write if they had the time and clarity to say it well themselves.

## The tells

These are the patterns that make writing read as machine-generated. None of them are wrong in isolation — the problem is that a model reaches for all of them, all the time, and the reader feels the sameness.

**Em-dash overload.** This is the single most common tell, and the easiest one to fall back into out of habit — it's the default connector a model reaches for when it hasn't decided whether two clauses want a period, a comma, or a colon. Treat the default as zero, not "one or two." Before finalizing any document, go back through the actual draft and find every em dash specifically — don't rely on having "written carefully" the first time through, since the habit is exactly what needs catching. Replace each one with a period, a comma, or a colon depending on what the sentence needs. Only leave one in if the interruption is genuinely the point — a real aside or a hard pivot mid-thought, not just a place a pause felt natural. More than one in a short document is a sign the em dash is being used as filler rather than a deliberate choice.

**The "not just X, it's Y" contrast frame.** "This isn't just a résumé tool — it's a philosophy on how work should be tailored." This construction sounds insightful the first time and hollow the fifth. If the contrast isn't doing real work, cut it and just state the thing.

**Rule of three.** "Fast, scalable, and reliable." "Design, build, and ship." Three-item lists feel complete and rhythmic, which is exactly why they're overused — real thoughts don't usually come in neat triplets. Vary the count: sometimes one thing matters, sometimes five.

**Corporate/LinkedIn buzzwords.** Leverage, robust, seamless, cutting-edge, passionate, proven track record, extensive experience, dynamic, results-driven. These are vague by design — they let you avoid saying the specific thing. Replace each with the actual detail it's standing in for.

**Symmetrical hedging.** "On one hand X, but on the other hand Y" — used even when the writer clearly leans one way. If there's a real opinion or a real reason, say it plainly instead of performing balance.

**Formulaic openers.** "In today's fast-paced world..." / "As a [role] with a passion for [thing]..." / restating the question back before answering it. The reader already knows what role they're hiring for and what question they asked — spend the first sentence on content, not throat-clearing.

**Formulaic closers.** "I look forward to the opportunity to discuss further" / "Thank you for your time and consideration." These aren't bad because they're polite — they're bad because they're generic enough to append to literally any letter. A closer that references something specific from the letter itself reads as considered rather than templated.

**Transition-word padding.** Furthermore, moreover, additionally, in conclusion. If removing the transition word doesn't change the meaning, it wasn't doing anything.

**Uniform sentence rhythm.** AI prose tends toward medium-length sentences of similar structure, one after another. Real writing has some short, blunt sentences next to longer ones that wander a bit before landing. Rhythm variation is one of the strongest signals of a human hand.

## What replaces it

- **Specificity over abstraction.** "Extensive experience with distributed systems" tells the reader nothing. "Owned the architecture of a three-tier payments platform" tells them something. If a sentence could apply to almost anyone in the field, it needs a name, a number, or an artifact in it.
- **Let claims be asymmetric.** Not every point needs equal weight or a matching counterpart. Say the important thing at length and the minor thing in passing.
- **Cut the meta-commentary.** Sentences that describe what the letter is about to do ("I'm excited to share why I'd be a great fit") rarely survive contact with a good edit. Just do the thing.
- **End without over-summarizing.** A closing paragraph that restates everything already said reads as padding. Land on the most specific, forward-looking point instead.

## Before / after

**Cover letter opener**

Before: *"In today's fast-paced tech landscape, I am thrilled at the opportunity to apply for the Software Engineer role. With a proven track record of delivering robust, scalable solutions, I believe I would be a valuable addition to your team."*

After: *"I read your engineering blog post on the Triton migration twice before writing this — that's the kind of cost-latency tradeoff I spent the last year living inside of as a founding engineer."*

**Follow-up email closer**

Before: *"Thank you again for your time and consideration. I look forward to the opportunity to discuss this position further and am confident my skills would be a great asset to your team."*

After: *"The question about how you handle schema migrations across regions stuck with me — happy to talk through how I solved something similar if it'd be useful."*

## Pre-send checklist

This isn't a background intention to keep in mind while writing — it's a pass you run against the finished draft before sending it, because the patterns below are exactly the ones that slip through when you're focused on content instead. Read the actual text you're about to send and check each item against it:

1. Find every em dash in the draft and look at it individually. Zero is the expectation for a short document. For each one, replace it with a period, a comma, or a colon unless it's doing something a period genuinely couldn't — a real interruption, not just a convenient pause.
2. Scan for any list of exactly three adjectives or verbs. Break the rhythm — cut one, or make it a different length.
3. Does the opening sentence restate the prompt or the role? Delete it and start with the second sentence instead.
4. Does the closer say anything a form letter couldn't also say? If not, replace it with something specific to this document.
5. Search for: leverage, robust, seamless, passionate, proven track record, extensive experience, dynamic, cutting-edge, in today's. Replace each with the specific fact it's standing in for, or cut it.
6. Read it out loud. Any sentence that sounds like it's performing enthusiasm rather than stating a fact — rewrite it plainer.

## Scope

Apply this to cover letters, application essay answers, professional emails, and other documents meant to carry the user's voice to someone else. This is about the deliverable text itself — it's not a mandate to make ordinary conversational replies in chat sound stilted or overly literary; keep those natural and direct as usual.
